Part 1

The project in brief

What problem CallVerse solves, how the business objectives become data-science objectives, and how the five layers fit together.

1.1 The problem

An e-commerce contact centre fails in two ways: too few advisors during promotions or delivery incidents (customers wait, hang up, complain) and too many when it is quiet (money wasted). Decisions — how many people to schedule, who takes which customer, what an advisor may promise — are usually made from intuition and tested directly on real customers.

CallVerse is a digital twin: a virtual copy of the contact centre where every decision is tested first. It forecasts how many customers will contact the centre, plans the shifts, simulates every customer and every advisor minute by minute, lets AI agents answer and grade conversations, and compares every idea with a baseline using confidence intervals.

1.2 From business objectives to data-science objectives

The framework follows a strict chain: BO (business objective) → KPI → DSO (data-science objective) → metric. Each DSO has a main idea, a baseline it must beat, a backup, and a switch rule that decides automatically which one is kept.

Part 2

How everything works

Every concept used in CallVerse, in the order data flows through the system: from raw orders to a forecast, from a forecast to a staffing plan, from a plan to a simulated day, and from a customer message to a graded AI answer.

2.1 Data layer

Why synthetic contacts

No public dataset contains real contact-centre logs linked to orders. CallVerse therefore starts from two real public datasets and generates the contacts with explicit, documented assumptions (all marked is_synthetic = True):

  • Olist — 99,441 real orders of a Brazilian marketplace (2016–2018): purchase, approval, carrier and delivery timestamps, estimated delivery date, payments, items, reviews, customer city.
  • Bitext — real customer-service utterances labelled by intent. 17 order-related intents are kept and grouped into four problem families: wismo (where is my order), cancel_refund, post_delivery (damaged, wrong, missing items, returns) and order_admin (invoices, address changes, payment).

How a contact is generated

  1. Each order is inspected for situations that make customers write: delivery late, seller shipped late, order canceled, payment anomaly, low review score, long transit…
  2. Each situation has a contact probability and a timing (days after an anchor event, e.g. 2 days after the shipping limit), set in configs/data.yaml. A random draw (fixed seed) decides whether the customer contacts the centre.
  3. The contact receives an intent consistent with the situation and the real wording of a Bitext message of that intent.
  4. Promotion windows (Black Friday, Christmas) and public holidays shift the volumes.

Result: 9,179 contacts (Jan 2017 → Aug 2018), each linked to a real order. From them, 9,179 tickets and 60,837 chat messages are generated: a rules engine decides the policy-correct outcome (tracking shared, refund approved, carrier investigation, hand-over to logistics…), approved answer templates write the advisor turns, and a 30% CSAT survey is simulated.

SQLite store and knowledge base

  • SQLite (data/callverse.db) holds orders, tickets and chats with keys; the advisor's tools query it "as of" the moment the customer writes, so an order that was "shipped" on that day is never shown as "delivered".
  • Knowledge base: 10 hand-written policy documents (refunds, returns, delays, invoices, payments, cancellations, tracking, FAQ, damaged items, advisor playbook). Each "##" section becomes a chunk (58 chunks), embedded with bge-m3 (multilingual, 1024-dimension vectors) and stored in a FAISS exact inner-product index. Vectors are L2-normalised, so the inner product equals the cosine similarity.
  • Pandera contracts check every processed table (types, ranges, uniqueness, allowed values) after each pipeline run, before the forecast and the twin read the data.
  • The data notebooks (03–08) stay the single source of truth: callverse data re-executes them headless (nbclient).

2.2 Demand forecast (DSO4, part 1)

Target: daily number of contacts per problem family, forecast 1 to 7 days ahead (the planning horizon of a weekly roster).

Features — leakage-safe

Every lag is ≥ 7 days, so a forecast made on Sunday for the next Saturday only uses information available on Sunday: contacts 7, 14, 21, 28 days before; orders, deliveries, late deliveries and overdue backlog with the same lags; day of week, promotion calendar, public holidays. A unit test checks that lag_7 equals the value 7 days earlier.

Model and baselines

  • Main: one global LightGBM model for the four groups (the group is a feature), with a Poisson objective because the target is a count.
  • Baselines: seasonal naive ("same day last week", the DSO4 baseline), 4-week weekday mean, SARIMA, Prophet.

Rolling-origin backtest

Every Sunday from October 2017 to August 2018 (46 origins), each model is trained only on the past and forecasts the next 7 days. Errors are aggregated over all origins:

wMAPE = Σ |actual − forecast| / Σ actual     MAE = mean |error|     RMSE = √ mean(error²)     bias = Σ(forecast − actual) / Σ actual

wMAPE is the decision metric: unlike MAPE it does not explode on days with very few contacts. Result on the total: LightGBM 25.3% vs 31.6% (naive), 27.7% (SARIMA), 31.9% (4-week mean), 35.9% (Prophet). Small groups (a few contacts a day) are close to the Poisson noise floor, so the total is the decision level. Every run is logged to MLflow.

2.3 Queueing theory: Erlang C

A contact centre is a queue with c servers (advisors). With arrival rate λ (contacts per hour) and average handle time AHT, the offered load is A = λ · AHT (in Erlangs: the number of advisors that would be busy all the time). The Erlang C formula gives the probability that a customer has to wait:

P(wait) = [ Ac/c! · c/(c−A) ] / [ Σk=0..c−1 Ak/k! + Ac/c! · c/(c−A) ]
Service level = 1 − P(wait) · e−(c−A)·T/AHT     ASA = P(wait) · AHT / (c − A)

CallVerse uses it three ways: (1) the required number of agents per hour is the smallest c reaching the 80/20 target (80% of chats answered within 20 s) with occupancy ≤ 90%; (2) the Workforce agent quotes the expected wait of a conversation; (3) it validates the simulator: on a stationary hour the SimPy engine must reproduce Erlang C (error < 4%).

Little's law (L = λ · W: average number waiting = arrival rate × average wait) explains why the routing reward "minus customer-seconds of waiting" is the same as minimising the average wait.

2.4 The digital twin (DSO4, part 2)

The twin is a discrete-event simulation written with SimPy: time jumps from event to event (an arrival, an advisor becoming free, a customer hanging up, a shift starting) instead of ticking every second.

Ingredients

ElementHow it is modelled
ArrivalsDaily volumes per group from the history or the scenario, spread over the day with the hourly profile, as a Poisson process (random but realistic bursts).
Live vs asynchronousChats arriving during opening hours (8:00–22:00) are live; messages arriving at night form an overnight backlog worked first in the morning.
CustomersPersonas: impatient 25% (patience ×0.5), normal 55%, patient 20% (×2). A customer whose patience runs out abandons (hangs up). Repeat contacts are more often impatient.
Handle timeLognormal per problem family (long right tail).
AdvisorsEach has a main skill: ×0.75 handle time on their specialty, ×1.3 on weak groups. A duty matrix (agent × hour) says who works when.
OvertimePlanned overtime hours, plus a real-time rule: if the queue exceeds 8 customers, 2 on-call agents join.
RoutingA router decides which waiting customer a free advisor takes: FIFO, skill score or PPO (section 2.6).
Costs€12/h regular, overtime ×1.5, €8 per lost (abandoned) customer.

KPIs

Service level (answered within 20 s), ASA (average speed of answer), mean wait of all live customers, p90 wait, abandon rate, occupancy, understaffed hours (scheduled < Erlang C requirement), overtime hours, labour cost, cost per contact, total cost.

Scenarios and "prepared vs unprepared"

Scenarios (configs/scenarios.yaml) are real weeks with multipliers: promo spike, delivery delays, staff shortage (absences), Black Friday 2017. A plan is unprepared when it is built from the normal forecast and prepared when it is built after testing the scenario in the twin. Comparing both on the same customers measures the value of testing first (BO4).

Statistics: common random numbers and paired confidence intervals

Each option is simulated over 10 seeds. For a given seed, every option sees exactly the same customers (same arrival times, same patience, same handle times): this is the common random numbers technique. The difference between two options is computed seed by seed, and a paired t-interval gives its 95% confidence interval. A difference is "significant" when the interval does not contain 0. Pairing removes the noise of the customers themselves, so small effects become measurable.

Fidelity: is the twin realistic?

  • Kolmogorov–Smirnov test on daily volumes, simulated vs historical: p = 0.998 (no detectable difference).
  • Hourly profile: total-variation distance 0.015 between simulated and historical shares per hour.
  • Erlang C check: on stationary hours, simulated P(wait) and ASA within 4% of the formula.

2.5 Workforce agent — shift planning (DSO3)

Given the hourly requirement (Erlang C on the forecast), the planner chooses who works when. It is an integer optimisation solved with Google OR-Tools CP-SAT in two stages:

  1. Coverage: choose how many people start each of 27 shift templates (4, 6 or 8 hours) each day, plus overtime hours (≤ 4 per hour, ≤ 60 per week), minimising regular cost + 1.5 × overtime cost + 200 × understaffed agent-hours.
  2. Roster: assign the chosen shifts to named agents under hard labour rules: at most 5 days and 40 hours a week, one shift per day, at least 11 hours of rest between shifts.

The solver proves optimality in under a second. It runs with num_workers = 1: with 8 parallel workers it returned different (equally optimal) rosters from run to run, which moved the simulation KPIs — determinism matters more than speed here.

Baselines and backups: fixed schedule sized for the peak (same staff every hour), fixed tuned (sized at the 75% quantile), Erlang C + rules (morning/afternoon templates + overtime rule), Erlang C hourly (theoretical reference).

2.6 Workforce agent — real-time routing (DSO1)

When an advisor becomes free, the router chooses which waiting customer they take.

  • FIFO with skill matching (baseline): oldest customer first.
  • Skill score (backup): a weighted score of waiting time, the advisor's speed on that problem, persona and live/overnight; the weights are tuned by random search in the twin.
  • MaskablePPO (main idea): a reinforcement-learning policy trained only inside the simulator.

The RL problem (MDP)

PartDefinition
StateThe 10 oldest waiting customers, 12 features each (wait so far, problem family, persona, live or overnight, the free advisor's speed on that family…) + 7 global features (queue length, staff on duty, time of day…).
ActionWhich of the 10 slots the free advisor takes. Empty slots are masked (MaskablePPO never picks an invalid action).
Reward−(Δ customer-seconds of waiting + 600 × Δ abandons) / 600: every waiting second costs, a hang-up costs 10 minutes.
Training300,000 steps on single busy days (load ×1.0–1.8, group surges, 5–30% absences), 2017-02 → 2018-03; test on unseen days 2018-04 → 2018-08. Network 2 × 128, γ = 0.995, learning rate 3e-4, entropy 0.01.

The SimPy engine has a steppable mode (advance() / resolve()) wrapped as a Gymnasium environment, so the same simulator serves training, evaluation and the live floor.

Result: on unseen stressed days PPO reduces ASA by 13.9% versus FIFO; in full peak weeks (multi-day backlog, planned overtime) it does not reduce the mean wait (+2.2%) although it cuts long waits (p90 −18 to −25%). Following the switch rule, the tuned skill-score router is the production candidate; PPO stays the research track (next step: multi-day training episodes).

2.7 The conversational agents

Client agent (plays the customer)

It knows a profile: the real opening message, the order, the hidden situation, a persona and a tone. It answers the advisor with an LLM in JSON ({"message", "end", "csat"}) or with rules offline. Guardrails: verification questions ("your order number?") are always answered deterministically, and if the LLM starts writing like an advisor ("how can I help you?") the turn falls back to the rule-based customer. At the end it rates the chat 1–5 (CSAT).

Advisor agent: Ask → Retrieve → Decide → Reply

  1. Understand: an intent classifier (TF-IDF + logistic regression, 99.0% accuracy on held-out Bitext) predicts the intent.
  2. Retrieve: bge-m3 + FAISS returns the top-3 policy sections. Below a cosine of 0.57 the advisor says the knowledge base has no answer.
  3. Verify: no order data is shared before the customer gives an order number (playbook rule 1).
  4. Tools on the SQLite store, as of the contact time: lookup_order, get_ticket_history, check_refund_eligibility, open_ticket, search_knowledge_base and recommend_resolution (the company rules engine).
  5. Reply in plain language.
VariantHow it works
template (backup)Classifier + approved answers filled with the order facts. Deterministic, instant, used offline.
rag (pure idea)The LLM decides which tools to call and what to answer.
rag_policy (hybrid, production)As soon as the order number is known, the pipeline itself calls lookup_order and the rules engine; the LLM receives the decision ("action: carrier investigation, hand over to: logistics, approved answer: …") and only writes it. The rules engine decides, the LLM writes.

Guardrails added after the first LLM runs: tool gating (order tools exist only after an order number appears — small models called them with invented ids), and a verification guardrail (a reply given before verification always ends by asking for the order number).

Quality agent (the reviewer)

It fills a scorecard of five criteria (1–5): compliance (policy followed, no invented promise), relevance, satisfaction, sentiment, resolution, plus "correct" and "resolved" verdicts and a one-sentence rationale. It receives the policy-correct reference (what the rules engine says for the true intent). The LLM judge comes from a different model family than the advisor (IBM Granite vs Hugging Face SmolLM3) so it does not share its blind spots. Offline, a rule-based checklist fills the same scorecard.

Can the judge be trusted? Calibration

The judge's grades are compared with labels using Cohen's quadratic kappa (chance-corrected agreement on an ordinal scale, 1 = perfect, 0 = chance; disagreements of 2 points cost 4× more than 1 point) and Spearman's ρ. Rule: trusted at kappa ≥ 0.4. Without human labels, proxy labels come from the generator's answer key; once 20+ chats are graded by humans on the labeling page, the calibration switches to them automatically.

Workforce agent inside a conversation

For each chat it predicts the problem family, picks the best-skilled desk and quotes the Erlang C expected wait for that hour.

2.8 The local LLMs (Hugging Face)

RoleModelWhy
Advisor + simulated customerSmolLM3-3B (Hugging Face), 3.1 B parameters, Apache-2.0Fully open (weights, data, recipe), tool calling, strong for its size; one copy serves both roles.

Retrievalbge-m3 (BAAI), on CPUMultilingual embeddings; keeps the GPU for the LLMs.

Fitting two LLMs in a 4 GB laptop GPU

  • 4-bit NF4 quantization (bitsandbytes): each weight is stored in 4 bits using "NormalFloat" levels that match the bell-shaped distribution of neural-network weights; double quantization also compresses the scaling constants. A 3B model goes from ~6 GB (16-bit) to ~2 GB. Both models together use ~3.2 GB of the GTX 1650's 4 GB.
  • bfloat16 compute: the matrix products run in bf16. Float16 overflows on these models (activations above 65,504) and the output becomes "!!!!"; bf16 has the range of float32, and PyTorch emulates it on this Turing GPU at the same speed.
  • Speed: ~8–13 tokens/s; an advisor reply takes ~7–10 s, a whole four-agent conversation ~45 s.

From messages to tokens and back

  • Chat templates: each model has its own prompt format; apply_chat_template renders it. Tool schemas are passed the model's way (SmolLM3 uses xml_tools); system messages are merged into one; "thinking" mode is disabled for faster answers.
  • Tool-call parsing: the model writes <tool_call>{"name": …, "arguments": …}</tool_call>; the parser also accepts Granite's and bare-JSON formats and ignores unknown tools.
  • Streaming: a background thread generates while a TextIteratorStreamer yields text pieces; a stream gate forwards them to the UI but hides tool-call markup (it holds back the last 12 characters until it is sure they do not start a tool call).
  • JSON outputs (client turns, judge scorecards): generation stops as soon as the JSON object is closed; a repair step closes an object that a small model left unfinished.
  • Cache: every call is keyed by the SHA-256 of (model, commit, messages, tools, temperature, seed); an identical call is answered from SQLite instantly, which makes runs reproducible.
  • Tracing: every call is appended to llm_calls.jsonl (agent, model, tokens, latency, tokens/s, tools, cache hit).
  • Pinning: callverse llm-pull downloads the models and writes the exact Hub commit of each repository to llm_models.lock.json; afterwards models load from the local cache only.
  • Graceful degradation: with no CUDA GPU or no downloaded weights, every agent switches to its offline backup and the UI says so.

LangGraph runs this as an explicit state machine; the conditional edge after client_reply loops back to the advisor until the customer leaves (or 4 customer turns). The final KPI row holds: first-contact resolution, CSAT, compliant (compliance ≥ 4), policy-correct action (advisor's outcome = reference outcome), judge verdict, tool success rate, intent predicted, turns, expected wait. Batches of conversations (callverse agents) produce the DSO2 tables.

Live sessions: a human on either side

orchestration/live.py drives the same agents one step at a time, so a human can play the customer (front-office chat) or the advisor (advisor desk, with the AI as copilot or autopilot). The browser talks to it through the WebSocket /ws/session:

2.10 Evaluation framework

  • KPI engine (evaluation/business_kpis.py): means, 95% CIs and paired differences of every KPI.
  • Experiments: DSO3 planners vs fixed; DSO1 routers vs FIFO (stressed days and peak weeks); BO4 prepared vs unprepared; DSO2 retrieval (255 Bitext questions: recall@1, recall@4, MRR), conversations per variant, judge calibration, faithfulness and similarity to the policy answer.
  • Component metrics: is each part good on its own (forecast wMAPE, retrieval recall, judge kappa, twin KS and Erlang C error)?
  • Scorecard: applies each DSO's switch rule and writes the verdicts (scorecard.json), shown in both apps.
  • BO4 money: saving = (unprepared − prepared) labour cost + €8 × lost customers avoided; the break-even value of a lost customer is the value above which preparing pays off (€6–12 depending on the scenario).
MRR = mean(1 / rank of the first correct section)     recall@k = share of questions whose correct section is in the top k

2.12 Frontend architecture

  • npm workspaces: frontend/shared (design system), front-office, back-office. Both apps are React 18 + TypeScript + Vite; the shared package is consumed as source, so a component change shows in both apps at once.
  • Design tokens: colours are CSS variables with a light and a dark set; Tailwind classes point to them, so themes switch without rebuilding. Chart colours use a validated colour-blind-safe palette with a fixed colour per entity (e.g. "Where is my order" is always blue).
  • Motion (Framer Motion): page transitions, staggered entrances, count-up numbers, animated rings and bars, spring chat bubbles, an animated agent pipeline. The OS "reduce motion" setting is respected; ?snapshot renders every animation in its final state.
  • Live floor: drawn on an HTML <canvas> at 60 fps (hundreds of moving customers would be too slow as DOM nodes).
  • Live session client: a React hook (useLiveSession) turns WebSocket events into state (messages, streaming text, active agent, stages, routing, suggestion, review) through one reducer.
  • Quality of life: command palette (Ctrl+K), toasts, skeleton loaders, error boundaries (one broken view never blanks the app), self-hosted fonts (works offline on demo day).

2.13 MLOps and software quality

  • Reproducibility: seeds in configs, pinned model commits, LLM cache, CP-SAT single worker, callverse all rebuilds everything.
  • Docker Compose: api, web (one nginx serving the front office on 8080 and the back office on 8081, proxying /api and /ws), mlflow + postgres; docker-compose.gpu.yml adds CUDA PyTorch and the NVIDIA GPU.

3.2 Semantic search

  1. The query is embedded with bge-m3 and compared with the 58 policy sections in FAISS (debounced 280 ms after the last keystroke).
  2. Each result shows its cosine similarity (bar + value), the section title, the article and a snippet with the query words highlighted.
  3. The footer names the retriever and the search latency. Keyboard: arrows to move, Enter to open the article at that exact section.
Why semantic and not keyword search?"my parcel is late and nobody answers" shares almost no word with "Order late: estimated date has passed", yet it is the right section. On 255 Bitext questions, bge-m3 finds the correct section in the top 4 for 96.9% of questions, against 80.4% for TF-IDF keyword search (the DSO2 baseline).

D. Honest limits

  • Contacts, tickets and chats are synthetic (generated from real orders and real wording with explicit assumptions); real logs would replace them without changing the pipeline.
  • Small 3B/1.6B models on a laptop: the hybrid design is mandatory, and the LLM judge is not yet trusted (kappa < 0.4); 60 human labels are the next step.
  • PPO is trained on single days; multi-day episodes are needed to beat FIFO on mean wait in full peak weeks.
  • The DSO2 conversation numbers in the report predate the switch to Hugging Face models; the re-run is planned.
